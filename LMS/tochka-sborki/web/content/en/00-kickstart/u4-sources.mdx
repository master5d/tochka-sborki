<Phase type="activation">

Recall the last time you came across a promise to teach you AI — a post, an ad, someone's channel. Don't judge it, just bring it back to mind.

Now picture yourself at a shelf in a shop, holding a jar. You don't trust the picture on the front — you turn the jar around and look at the ingredients, the expiry date, and who made it. Three glances, five seconds, decision made.

AI materials have exactly the same back of the jar. Run it through your head: what would you want to read there before handing that source a week of your life?

</Phase>

<Phase type="reflection">

You've just felt the difference between **the packaging** and **the ingredients**.

Packaging can be honest: good material has a nice cover too. The point isn't to distrust everything — blanket suspicion costs you as much time as blind trust. The point is to have a five-second check you run every time.

Picture this skill as the habit of glancing at an expiry date: you don't think about it, it's simply there. Catch this in your head: how many of the sources sitting in your bookmarks right now would survive that check?

</Phase>

<Phase type="concept">

## Three glances at the back of the jar

Everything worth checking about open material is on its page and takes seconds.

**1. The licence — what you're allowed to do**

These are the ingredients. Genuinely open material has a licence file, and that file grants you rights: MIT and Apache-2.0 let you read, copy, modify and use it in your own projects. Creative Commons lets you read and share, sometimes with strings attached. `CC BY-NC` means no commercial use. No licence at all doesn't mean "anything goes" — it means "nothing goes": by default the rights stay with the author.

If material is being sold and there's no licence anywhere, you aren't buying knowledge — you're buying access. That's legal, but it's a different product.

**2. The date of the last change — the expiry date**

On GitHub it's right there: when the last commit landed. In a field where tools shift every month, material from two years ago describes a world that no longer exists.

Look at the date of the last **edit**, not the date of publication. A course released in 2024 and maintained since is more alive than one released yesterday and abandoned right after launch.

**3. The author — who made it**

An organisation or a person with a track record? Is other work of theirs nearby? Can you see the code, or only the story about it? In open projects the whole history is visible: who committed, how they answered questions, what they fixed.

A separate signal is **how many people look at it and fix it**. Not follower count — participation: forks, issues, discussions. Material nobody has checked may be excellent, but you'll be its first tester.

## A live example: audit our own recommendation

The course materials link to Microsoft's agents course. Check it yourself, right now, and see what the three glances return:

```
Licence .......... MIT — take it, modify it, use it
Last commit ...... every week
Author ........... Microsoft, the repository is fully open
Participation .... tens of thousands of stars, hundreds of forks
```

It passes. And it still has a weak spot that the same three glances expose: the Russian version was produced by a machine translator — stated honestly at the top of every file. A good source doesn't mean flawless in every part. That's exactly why we point you to the English original and wrote the lesson map in our own words.

## What the check won't tell you

It doesn't say whether the material is any good. A live repository under an open licence can be useless, and a closed paid course can be excellent. The check answers a different question: **what happens if the author disappears.**

For an open source, the answer is nothing: the material stays with you, you can copy it and carry on yourself. For gated access, the answer is that you lose everything you paid for.

That's sovereignty applied to learning: not "never pay anyone", but **never be left with nothing** when someone else's platform shuts down.

<Phase type="practice">

Open three sources you're currently learning from, or planned to — any kind: a repository, a course, a channel, a newsletter.

Run each through the three glances:

```
Source: [name and link]

Licence .......... [which one / couldn't find it]
Last updated ..... [date / not visible]
Author ........... [who, what else they've made]

If the author disappears: [I keep it / I lose access]
```

Save it to `my-experiments/u4-sources-check.md`.

One of the three probably won't pass. That's not a reason to throw it away — it's a reason not to build your foundation on it.

<Phase type="activation">

In unit 1 you may have caught the moment: a video with no subtitles — and the notebook answered about it anyway. Confidently, fluently, with details.

Where did the details come from, if there was nothing to read?

Think of a salesman who does not know the answer to your question but badly wants the sale. He does not go silent — he says something plausible. The tone is confident, the words are smooth, and the less he knows, the smoother it comes out.

</Phase>

<Phase type="reflection">

Keep an uncomfortable thought in mind: **confidence and truth are not connected.** At all.

A tool that is obliged to answer will answer — and where the source is empty, it fills the gap with the most plausible thing. Not out of malice: plausibility is its craft.

Your defense is not "trust less" (distrusting everything paralyzes exactly like credulity). Your defense is a check that takes a minute and happens every time. Like the three looks at the can from module 0 — now for answers instead of sources.

</Phase>

<Phase type="concept">

## Six places where the notebook drifts

The checklist from the [notebook pack](/en/notebook/) — each item unpacked:

**1. A citation that does not lead to the source.** Click two or three citations in any answer that matters. The link must land where the phrase actually stands. If it lands "roughly there" or in a different paragraph — you are looking at a retelling wearing a citation's costume.

**2. A confident answer with no citation at all.** Smooth text without footnotes is a signal: the notebook drifted from your sources into general knowledge. Re-ask with the "Questions with citations" prompt — it forbids answering without evidence.

**3. Numbers and percentages.** This is where retellings drift most: "about 40%" becomes "nearly half," "in one experiment" becomes "as a rule." Verify every number against the source with its own click.

**4. An answer beyond the sources.** Ask a control question about something you know is not in the sources. An honest notebook says "the sources do not cover this." If a fluent answer comes back instead — you just learned the price of every other answer in the session.

**5. One source presented as consensus.** "Research shows" — and the stack holds a single article. Ask directly: which of the sources actually claims this? One voice is one voice, however convincing.

**6. An empty source.** A video without subtitles, a corrupted PDF, a paywalled page. The notebook will not always tell you a source is empty — it may "answer" from the title and its general knowledge. Your exhibit from unit 1 is exactly this case.

## This is not a bug report — it is literacy

Notice: not one item requires technical knowledge. All of it is reader's habits: click, verify a number, ask a control question. The same skill a fact-checking editor has — the tool is just newer.

And the reverse: without these habits the notebook is dangerously good. It produces the form of credibility — footnotes, structure, calm tone — faster than you can check the substance. Form without checking is packaging. And you already know what to do with packaging.

</Phase>

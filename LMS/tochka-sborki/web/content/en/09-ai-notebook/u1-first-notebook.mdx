<Phase type="activation">

Take a rough count: how many hours of video are sitting in your "watch later" right now? A playlist of talks, a couple of lectures, something a friend sent with "you have to see this."

Now honestly: how much of it will you actually watch?

Picture a different deal. Same evening, same videos — but instead of a screen you have a digest: the key claims, the places where speakers disagree, and next to every claim a link to the exact spot where it was said. Not a retelling of who-knows-what, but a map with coordinates.

</Phase>

<Phase type="reflection">

Notice what changed in that picture. It is not "someone watched it for me" — it is that **every claim got an address**.

You cannot ask a retelling "where did you get that?" A digest with addresses can take the question: click and check. The gap between "I was told" and "here is the spot where it was said" is the same gap between opinion and source that module 0 started with.

Weigh it up: which of your postponed videos would you check first?

</Phase>

<Phase type="concept">

## Build first, understand second

This module runs in reverse order: you get a working notebook within 15 minutes, and "how it works" plus "where it lies" come in the next units. That is the honest order: arguing with a tool is worth doing after it is in your hands.

The unit's tool is **Gemini Notebook** (formerly NotebookLM, notebooklm.google.com): the free tier is enough, no payment needed. It is not a chat: the notebook answers **only from the sources you put into it**, and attaches a citation linking into the source for every claim.

No notebook? Not a blocker: the same prompts work in any chat that accepts file attachments. Citations will not be clickable, so you verify by searching the text — slower, but the principle holds.

## The fifteen minutes

**1. Open the [notebook pack](/en/notebook/)** — three ready-made packs. Take the first one: "A playlist instead of an evening of watching."

**2. Create a notebook and add sources.** Three to five videos from your "watch later" — one link each. The notebook reads subtitles, not video: a clip without subtitles stays an empty source (this matters — unit 3 comes back to it).

**3. Ask your first question** — not "summarize," but the one from the prompt kit: "Where do these videos disagree with each other?" Disagreements beat summaries: a summary averages, a disagreement shows you where to think for yourself.

**4. Demand citations.** Take the "Questions with citations" prompt from the pack — it requires a citation for every claim and forbids answering from memory when the sources do not contain the answer.

**5. Click two citations.** Make sure the link lands where the phrase was actually said. That is your first act of verification — the habit unit 3 will grow into a full checklist.

Done. You hold a digest of an evening of video, built in a quarter of an hour — and every claim in it has an address.

</Phase>

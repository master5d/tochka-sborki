<Phase type="activation">

The same material can come to you five ways: a text digest, a comparison table, an audio overview in your headphones, a mind map, slides.

Recall how you prepared for your last important conversation — an interview, a defense, a negotiation. What form did you need the material in back then? Now recall your commute or workout: would the same form have worked there?

Form is not decoration. Form is the answer to "where and why will I consume this."

</Phase>

<Phase type="reflection">

Notice the trap every tool with buttons pulls you toward: pressing all of them.

Generate the audio, the slides, the map, and the infographic — because you can. A week later you own five artifacts about a topic you never understood: generation took the place of understanding.

Hold the opposite principle: **first the question "what will I do with this," then the button.** A format with no consumption scenario is digital clutter with a nice cover.

</Phase>

<Phase type="concept">

## When each format is the honest one

**Table** — when sources disagree. Comparing positions row by row forces disagreements into the open; prose smooths them over. The prompt in the [pack](/en/notebook/) requires a "quote" column — so every cell has an address.

**Text digest with citations** — when you need to master the material and cite it downstream: at work, in a text, in an argument. The only format where verifying citations is built into reading.

**Audio overview** — when you have "dead" time: commute, workout, dishes. The honest use is a first pass over an unfamiliar topic, to decide whether it deserves a deep dive. The dishonest use is the only pass over a topic you will decide on: you cannot click a citation in audio. And always set a focus ("concentrate on X") — an unfocused overview averages the material into an even hum.

**Mind map** — when a topic has sprawled and lost its structure: what connects to what, where the clusters are, where the edges run. A map is good for navigation and nearly useless for retention — it is a table of contents, not a textbook.

**Slides** — when the material must be handed to others. For your own learning, slides almost always lose to a digest: they compress into bullet points exactly what you needed in detail.

## The one-format rule

One session — one format, chosen for the scenario. A second scenario appears ("understood the topic, now brief the team") — then a second format.

And every format gets the unit-3 inspection. A slide without a footnote and an audio claim without a source are harder to verify than text — which is why the pack's prompts demand evidence in every format, audio included.

</Phase>

<Phase type="practice">

Take a topic from your notebook and play a format match:

```
Topic: [...]
Consumption scenario: [where, when, why]
Chosen format: [...] — why this one and not the others: [...]

Generated. Inspection:
- evidence in place? [citations/footnotes/sources inside the format]
- did the format serve the scenario? [yes/no, what failed]

The format I did NOT generate and do not miss: [...]
```

Save to `my-experiments/u4-format-match.md`.

The last line is the important one: a button consciously not pressed is worth more than three pressed.

</Phase>
